Income versus cash, quarter by quarter

Operating income comes from the budgeted income statement. Net cash flow is the quarter’s cash collected minus everything paid out, before any borrowing. The gap between the two is the chapter: collections and payments lag, inventory is paid for as it is made but expensed when it sells, depreciation is an expense but never a check, and the equipment is a check but not an expense.

What the model assumes

Q1 matches the lecture exactly. Each case sells for $20 and needs 15 lbs of sugar at $0.20 a lb; ending raw materials are 10% of next quarter’s needs. Direct labor is 0.40 hours a case at $15 an hour. Overhead is $4.00 per labor hour plus $60,600 a quarter, of which $15,000 is depreciation. Selling and administrative costs are $1.80 a case plus $30,000 a quarter, of which $4,000 is depreciation. Depreciation is never a cash payment, so it stays out of the cash budget. The only equipment purchase is Q1’s $50,000; there are no dividends or taxes. Hampton starts the year with $40,000 of cash, $90,000 of receivables (all collected in Q1), $25,800 of payables (all paid in Q1), 2,000 cases and 21,000 lbs on hand. It keeps at least $30,000 of cash, borrows in $10,000 steps at the start of a quarter, and repays in $10,000 steps at the end of a quarter when cash allows, paying 1% a month interest on whatever it repays. Next year’s first quarter sells and produces 15,000 cases (25,000 when the season is flattened). Operating income is before interest; each case sold carries the budgeted $13.00 product cost, however many cases are made.
